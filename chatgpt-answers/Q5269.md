# Executive verdict

For an axiom-free Lean proof, there is no known shortcut of type (a), (b), or (c) that is genuinely simpler than the prime-level formal-immersion argument.

The precise answers are:

1. Faltings does not suffice. It proves that each fixed X₁(p)(ℚ) is finite once the genus is at least two, but gives neither the list of points nor a terminating enumeration procedure. In Lean, a proof of Set.Finite (X₁(p)(ℚ)) gives no way to conclude that every point is a cusp.

1. A pure local obstruction is impossible. For every rational prime ℓ and every integer n ≥ 1, there is a split Tate curve over ℚ_ℓ with a ℚ_ℓ-rational point of exact order n. In particular, Y₁(p)(ℚ_ℓ) is nonempty for every p and every ℓ—and this is true for n = 49 as well.

1. A finite-reduction theorem exists, and is much stronger than “check up to 97.” Merel–Oesterlé give, for a prime-order point over a degree-d number field,

For d = 1, this is p < 8, hence p ≤ 7. Thus it immediately excludes every prime p ≥ 11. But the Eisenstein-free proof uses the winding quotient, Hecke operators, modular symbols, formal immersion, and Kolyvagin–Logachev finiteness. It is a stronger theorem, not a simpler formalization target.[1]

1. Mazur’s rational-isogeny classification gives another finite reduction, because rational p-torsion implies a rational cyclic p-isogeny. For p ≥ 23, it leaves p ∈ {37,43,67,163}. However, that classification is itself essentially the same depth as the desired tail, and its obvious cutoff is 163, not 97.[2]

For this project, the cleanest serious route remains a narrow, reusable formalization of Mazur’s cusp/formal-immersion package, with the Eisenstein quotient arithmetic isolated behind a small interface. The winding-quotient route is the only clear answer to “can one avoid the Eisenstein ideal?”, but it is a poor answer to “can one make the Lean proof simpler?”

# 1. Repository-specific diagnosis

The current zinan-memory state records no_prime_order_ge_23 as the remaining formal-immersion tail, separate from the explicit 13, 17, 19, 25, and 49 obstructions. That separation is mathematically appropriate:

- the small orders can be attacked by explicit Tate-normal-form equations, descent, factorization, or finite residue calculations;

- the infinite family of prime levels needs a uniform modular-curve argument;

- trying to extend the explicit-polynomial style to every p merely recreates the uniform-boundedness problem in a less structured form.

There is also a useful correction to the four-step summary in the question. The fact that X₀(p) has genus at least two is not the operative input in the formal-immersion proof. What one actually needs is:

1. the modular point attached to the torsion pair specializes to a cusp at a small prime;

1. a map from X₀(p) to an abelian quotient A_p, normalized to send that cusp to zero;

1. enough finiteness or torsion control on A_p(ℚ) to force the modular point and cusp to have the same image;

1. formal immersion at the cusp, which then forces equality of the two points in the same residue disk;

1. a contradiction because the original modular point was noncuspidal.

Likewise, one does not need the quotient to “absorb all rational cuspidal torsion.” The minimum useful statement is that the relevant cusp difference survives in a quotient whose rational Mordell–Weil group is sufficiently controlled, and that the induced map is formally immersive. Mazur’s Eisenstein-ideal analysis is what supplies those facts uniformly.[3]

# 2. Why Faltings does not turn into an enumeration

For prime p ≥ 5, the standard genus formula is

Thus X₁(13) already has genus 2, X₁(23) has genus 12, and X₁(97) has genus 345. The genus formula is classical; a recent systematic account of modular-curve genus formulas is available here.[4]

Faltings proves that a smooth projective curve of genus greater than one over a number field has only finitely many rational points.[5] That conclusion is logically far weaker than what the torsion proof requires:

```plain text
Faltings:
    X₁(p)(ℚ) is finite.

Needed here:
    every element of X₁(p)(ℚ) is a cusp.
```

The gap is not a Lean technicality. Faltings’ theorem does not provide an effective height bound from which one can search all rational points. There is no known unconditional general algorithm that takes an arbitrary genus-at-least-two curve over ℚ and is guaranteed to output its complete rational-point set.

For a particular curve, one can sometimes prove completeness using:

- a Mordell–Weil computation for its Jacobian;

- Chabauty–Coleman when the Jacobian rank is below the genus;

- a Mordell–Weil sieve;

- quadratic or nonabelian Chabauty;

- explicit descent and local information.

These are additional theorems and computations, not consequences of finiteness. The Mordell–Weil sieve, for example, explicitly starts from global Jacobian information and combines it with reductions; it is not a local-solubility test.[6]

The Lean manifestation is especially stark. Even if one had

```javascript
hfinite : Set.Finite (X1Point p ℚ)
```

one could at most obtain a noncomputable finite container by classical choice. There would still be no theorem identifying its elements, no decidable search bound for their rational coordinates, and no route to

```javascript
∀ x : X1Point p ℚ, IsCusp x
```

without doing the missing arithmetic geometry. Formalizing Faltings would therefore add an enormous dependency while leaving the decisive statement unproved.

There is also no useful uniformity in p: Faltings applies separately to infinitely many different curves. Genus growth makes the one-curve-at-a-time strategy worse rather than better.

Verdict on (a): no. Faltings is a finiteness theorem, not an effective classification theorem, and it does not provide a Lean-checkable enumeration.

# 3. Why a pure local obstruction cannot exist

There is a decisive local counterexample.

Fix any rational prime ℓ and any integer n ≥ 1. In ℚ_ℓ, choose the Tate parameter

The split Tate curve satisfies

The class of ℓ has exact order n:

- its n-th power is ℓⁿ = q, hence trivial in the quotient;

- if 0 < k < n and ℓᵏ ∈ q^ℤ, taking ℓ-adic valuations would give k ∈ nℤ, impossible.

The Tate uniformization and its torsion description are standard; an explicit modern statement appears in Appendix A of Dokchitser–Dokchitser.[7]

Consequently,

```plain text
Y₁(n)(ℚ_ℓ) ≠ ∅
```

for every n and every finite place ℓ. In particular:

```plain text
Y₁(p)(ℚ_ℓ) ≠ ∅  for every prime p,
Y₁(49)(ℚ₂) ≠ ∅.
```

So no argument of the following form can prove the theorem:

```plain text
X₁(p)(ℚ_ℓ) has no noncuspidal point
therefore X₁(p)(ℚ) has no noncuspidal point.
```

The familiar small-prime reduction observation is weaker. If E/ℚ has good reduction at ℓ ≠ p, prime-to-ℓ torsion injects into E(𝔽_ℓ), and the Hasse bound makes a large p impossible. Hence a hypothetical large-torsion curve must have bad reduction at small primes such as 2 and 3. But split multiplicative reduction is exactly where the Tate-curve examples live. Local analysis forces the modular point into a cusp residue disk; it does not make the residue disk empty. The global Jacobian quotient plus formal immersion is what excludes a rational point inside that disk.

## Consequence for the order-49 work

This is a mandatory sanity check on the current 49 strategy. A valid mod-2 Tate-normal-form obstruction cannot be a theorem saying that no pointed elliptic curve over ℚ₂ has order 49; that statement is false.

Therefore a lemma such as

```plain text
preΨ'₄₉(0) ≠ 0 when b,c ∈ ℤ₂ and b is a unit
```

can exclude only a particular integral valuation branch. To turn it into a theorem for rational b,c, the normalization step must use a genuinely global restriction or must prove that every other valuation branch is impossible. A purely local change of variables cannot place every ℚ₂ Tate-normal-form pair into the unit branch, because the Tate curve with q=2⁴⁹ supplies a counterexample somewhere among the omitted branches.

Thus the order-49 proof may still be sound, but it is better described as a global-integrality argument tested at 2, not as local insolubility. The proof should make the valuation-branch reduction explicit in Lean.

Verdict on (b): no pure local obstruction. Individual global equations can sometimes be killed using a local residue calculation after imposing global normalization, but that does not scale to a uniform prime tail and cannot be justified by local emptiness of X₁(p).

# 4. Finite checking plus a uniform tail

## 4.1 Merel–Oesterlé: a decisive bound, but not a simpler proof

The strongest response to (c) is that a uniform cutoff already exists. Parent’s introduction records the Merel–Oesterlé prime-order bound

for a prime-order point over a degree-d number field. At d=1, the right side is approximately 7.46, so p ≤ 7.[1]

If this theorem were already available in Lean, the target would be almost trivial pseudocode:

```javascript
theorem no_prime_order_ge_23
    (p : ℕ) (hp : Nat.Prime p) (h23 : 23 ≤ p)
    (E : EllipticCurve ℚ) (P : E.Point)
    (hP : orderOf P = p) : False := by
  have hp7 : p ≤ 7 := merel_oesterle_prime_bound_degree_one E P hp hP
  omega
```

But formalizing merel_oesterle_prime_bound_degree_one is not a shortcut. Parent’s proof introduces:

- the Hecke algebra acting on modular symbols and J₀(pⁿ);

- the winding element and winding ideal;

- the winding quotient;

- finiteness of its rational points via Kolyvagin–Logachev;

- a formal-immersion criterion expressed as linear independence of Hecke translates of a modular symbol;

- a combinatorial estimate proving that criterion.

Parent explicitly states both that the winding quotient has finite rational points and that formal immersion is equivalent to a Hecke-linear-independence condition.[1]

This route does avoid Mazur’s Eisenstein ideal in the technical sense: it uses the winding ideal instead. However, for Lean it replaces one difficult algebraic package with a much larger analytic-arithmetic package. In particular, Kolyvagin–Logachev is far beyond the infrastructure needed for the narrow Mazur tail.

Parent also gives an explicit prime-power bound. At degree one and for primes other than 2,3, it specializes to

```plain text
pⁿ ≤ 65 · (3 − 1) · 2⁶ = 8320.
```

That can create a finite search only after the entire winding-quotient proof has been formalized. There is no special reason for a cutoff at 97.

## 4.2 Reduction through rational isogenies

A rational point of order p spans a Galois-stable cyclic subgroup, hence gives a rational p-isogeny. Mazur’s rational-isogeny theorem restricts the prime degree to

```plain text
{2,3,5,7,11,13,17,19,37,43,67,163}.
```

Therefore, conditional on that theorem, the p ≥ 23 tail reduces to

```plain text
{37,43,67,163}.
```

One would then inspect the rational points on the exceptional X₀(p) and show that their kernel character is nontrivial, so none lifts to a rational generator on X₁(p).

This is a legitimate finite architecture only if the rational-isogeny classification is already a trusted theorem in the development. As a replacement proof, it is circular in difficulty: Mazur’s isogeny classification is built from the same modular-curve, Hecke, and Eisenstein machinery. It is not a cheaper theorem to formalize merely because its conclusion is a finite list.

It also shows why 97 is not the natural cutoff: the isogeny list contains 163.

## 4.3 One curve at a time up to a bound

Suppose some independent uniform theorem supplied p ≤ B. One could in principle attack every X₁(p) for primes 23 ≤ p ≤ B. In practice this is unattractive:

- X₁(23) already has genus 12;

- by p=97, the genus is 345;

- their Jacobians have correspondingly large dimension;

- proving ranks, generators, saturation, and sieve completeness would require large certified computations for each level;

- an explicit affine equation and a Magma/Sage answer are not by themselves Lean certificates of all rational points.

A finite computational campaign is plausible only after reducing to a very small exceptional set, such as the four isogeny primes above, and only if the global rational-point information is exported as checkable algebraic certificates. It is not a credible replacement for the uniform theorem itself.

Verdict on (c): mathematically yes via Merel–Oesterlé, or conditionally via Mazur’s isogeny classification; formally no simplification.

# 5. The two genuine uniform routes

There are essentially two established uniform proof architectures relevant here.

## Route E: Mazur’s Eisenstein quotient

Core ingredients:

1. reduction of the modular point to a cusp at a small auxiliary prime;

1. a suitable Eisenstein quotient of J₀(p);

1. finiteness/torsion control of its rational points;

1. survival and order control of the cusp divisor;

1. the cotangent or q-expansion calculation giving formal immersion;

1. residue-disk rigidity.

Advantages for Lean:

- algebraic and integral rather than dependent on analytic rank and Euler systems;

- tailored exactly to the degree-one prime-level theorem;

- the finite calculations can be isolated as Hecke-algebra and q-expansion lemmas;

- it matches the project’s existing statement no_prime_order_ge_23 rather than proving a much more general theorem.

Disadvantage: it still requires a substantial first modular-curve/Jacobian/Hecke infrastructure.

## Route W: winding quotient / Merel–Oesterlé–Parent

Core ingredients:

1. winding element in modular-symbol homology;

1. Hecke annihilator and winding quotient;

1. Kolyvagin–Logachev finiteness of rational points;

1. Kamienny’s formal-immersion criterion;

1. modular-symbol linear independence and explicit estimates.

Advantage: it avoids the Eisenstein ideal and gives uniform boundedness over arbitrary number-field degree.

Disadvantage for this project: almost every major ingredient is absent and substantially more general than needed. The analytic-rank-to-Mordell–Weil-finiteness theorem is a particularly severe dependency.

Conclusion: Route W is the state-of-the-art affirmative answer to “avoid the Eisenstein ideal,” but Route E remains the better answer to “close this one Lean axiom concretely.”

# 6. Recommended Lean architecture

The proof should be split into one generic formal-geometry theorem and one modular arithmetic package. This avoids burying the project under a monolithic Mazur file.

## Layer A: generic formal-immersion rigidity

Prove a reusable theorem of the following mathematical shape.

Let R be a DVR with fraction field K and residue field k. Let X/R be a proper curve, A/R a separated group scheme or abelian scheme, and f : X → A. Let c,x ∈ X(K) extend to R-sections with the same special point. Assume:

- f(c)=0;

- the difference f(x)-f(c) is forced to be zero by rational-point finiteness plus torsion-specialization control;

- f_k is a formal immersion at the common special point.

Then x=c.

This is the geometric engine. It should know nothing about modular curves, Hecke operators, or Eisenstein ideals.

A schematic interface could be:

```javascript
theorem eq_of_same_reduction_of_formalImmersion
    (hsame : specialize x = specialize c)
    (himage : f x = f c)
    (hfi : FormalImmersionAt (specialFiberMap f) (specialize c)) :
    x = c
```

The exact types should follow the project’s scheme APIs, but the theorem boundary is stable.

## Layer B: prime-level modular reduction

Formalize only the moduli statement needed for a rational point P of prime order p:

- construct the associated noncuspidal point on X₁(p) and its image on X₀(p);

- extend it over the relevant localization/Néron model;

- use reduction type and small finite-field bounds to show that, after the standard quotient or Atkin–Lehner choice, it specializes to the designated cusp.

This layer should explicitly explain why good, additive, and nonsplit multiplicative cases are impossible or reducible, leaving the split multiplicative cusp case.

## Layer C: the minimal Eisenstein package

Do not initially formalize every theorem in Mazur’s 150-page paper. Define the smallest structure that the generic argument consumes. Mathematically it needs fields equivalent to:

```plain text
A_p                     an abelian quotient of J₀(p)
f_p : X₀(p) → A_p       normalized at the cusp
finite_Ap_Q              A_p(ℚ) is finite, or the exact torsion statement needed
cusp_survives            the relevant cusp divisor is not killed incorrectly
specialization_control   the needed torsion reduction map is injective
formal_immersion         f_p mod ℓ is formally immersive at the cusp
```

Then prove a generic theorem

```plain text
CuspExclusionPackage(p,ℓ)  ⇒  no rational point of order p.
```

Only after this interface compiles should the Eisenstein ideal be developed to construct the package uniformly for every prime p ≥ 23.

## Layer D: make finite arithmetic certificate-driven

Where the proof reduces to finite-dimensional Hecke or cotangent algebra, represent the data concretely:

- finite free modules with explicit bases;

- Hecke operators as integer matrices;

- quotient maps as matrices;

- mod-ℓ rank or determinant conditions;

- truncated q-expansions with a proved Sturm-type bound when needed.

Lean can then check rank and congruence claims by reflection or native_decide. The certificate must be accompanied by the theorem connecting that matrix computation to the geometric formal-immersion condition. Computation can discharge the finite algebra; it cannot replace the moduli/Jacobian theorem.

## Layer E: final tail theorem

The final file should be very small:

```javascript
theorem no_prime_order_ge_23
    (p : ℕ) (hp : Nat.Prime p) (h23 : 23 ≤ p)
    (E : EllipticCurve ℚ) (P : E.Point)
    (horder : orderOf P = p) : False := by
  let x := pointOnX0OfPrimeTorsion E P hp horder
  have hnoncusp : ¬ IsCusp x := ...
  have hred : specializesToInfinity x := primeTorsion_specializes_to_cusp ...
  have hx : x = cuspInfinity :=
    cuspExclusion_of_eisensteinPackage (eisensteinPackage p hp h23) x hred
  exact hnoncusp (hx ▸ cuspInfinity_isCusp)
```

This is pseudocode, not a claim about current identifiers. It shows the desired dependency direction: the tail theorem depends on one uniform package, not on a list of prime-specific polynomial files.

# 7. What not to pursue

1. Do not formalize Faltings for this purpose. Even a perfect formalization yields only finiteness and does not identify rational points.

1. Do not seek a place where X₁(p) is locally empty. Tate curves prove such a place does not exist.

1. Do not enumerate all primes through 97 without a prior uniform theorem. Genus and Jacobian dimension make this a poor certificate campaign, and 97 has no canonical mathematical role.

1. Do not replace the Eisenstein ideal with Kolyvagin–Logachev unless that analytic infrastructure is independently required elsewhere. This makes the dependency graph much larger.

1. Do not treat the order-49 mod-2 branch lemma as local insolubility. Audit the normalization and all valuation branches against the local Tate-curve counterexample.

# 8. Practical decision

The recommended priority is:

1. formalize the generic residue-disk/formal-immersion rigidity lemma;

1. formalize the prime-torsion-to-cusp-specialization lemma at small auxiliary primes;

1. state the exact minimal EisensteinTailPackage consumed by those lemmas;

1. build only enough modular-symbol/Hecke/cuspidal-divisor theory to construct that package uniformly;

1. keep Merel–Oesterlé as a comparison theorem, not as the implementation route;

1. use the rational-isogeny reduction only if the project later acquires Mazur’s full isogeny classification from another development.

This is still a substantial formalization, but it is the narrowest known path that actually proves the infinite tail rather than moving it behind a stronger theorem.

# State of formalization art

As of 2026, the public Lean benchmark for Mazur’s torsion theorem still lists it as unsolved and explicitly notes the absence of the required X₁(N) modular-curve infrastructure in Mathlib.[8] The present project’s explicit small-order progress is therefore genuinely ahead of the public baseline, but there is no existing library theorem that can be imported to close this tail.

# Bottom line

- (a) Faltings: no; finite is not enumerated, effective, or uniform.

- (b) local obstruction: no in the pure sense; local Tate curves have points of every order at every finite place.

- (c) finite checking plus a tail: yes only after importing a deep uniform theorem. Merel–Oesterlé kills the entire tail with p ≤ 7, but its Eisenstein-free proof is harder to formalize than the narrow Mazur argument. Mazur’s isogeny theorem reduces to {37,43,67,163}, but is not a cheaper dependency.

- Lean recommendation: formalize the generic formal-immersion rigidity theorem and then the smallest possible Eisenstein quotient package for prime level. That is the most concrete, checkable, and reusable route presently available.
