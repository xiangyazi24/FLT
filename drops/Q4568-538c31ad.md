# Explicit square-ideal proof for the cubic field

Let

\[
h(T)=T^3-4T^2+4T-2,
\qquad K=\mathbf Q(\alpha),
\qquad \mathcal O_K=\mathbf Z[\alpha],
\qquad h(\alpha)=0.
\]

For coprime integers \(x,z\), with \(z>0\), put

\[
\delta=x-24z^2+18z^2\alpha.
\]

Assume

\[
N_{K/\mathbf Q}(\delta)=y^2,
\qquad y\ne0.
\]

Then there is an integral ideal \(\mathfrak a\subseteq\mathcal O_K\) such that

\[
\boxed{(\delta)=\mathfrak a^2.}
\]

The proposed argument is essentially correct. Two qualifications are important:

1. for \(p\ne2,3\), the fixed residue of \(\alpha\) proves that there is a unique prime **among the primes over \(p\) that divide \((\delta)\)**; it does not claim that \(p\) has only one prime in \(K\);
2. odd residue degree alone is not enough if several primes over the same \(p\) divide \((\delta)\). The uniqueness statement is indispensable. The ramified split prime \(11\) is precisely the case where this distinction matters.

No further gap remains once those points are made explicit.

---

## 1. Fixed arithmetic of the field

The polynomial is irreducible over \(\mathbf Q\), and

\[
\operatorname{disc}(h)=-44=-2^2\cdot11.
\]

The assumption \(\mathcal O_K=\mathbf Z[\alpha]\) means the power basis has index one, so reduction of \(h\) modulo a rational prime gives the exact prime-ideal factorization by Dedekind's theorem.

### 1.1 Norm formula

For \(A,B\in\mathbf Z\), multiplication by \(A+B\alpha\) in the basis
\((1,\alpha,\alpha^2)\) has matrix

\[
M(A,B)=
\begin{pmatrix}
A&0&2B\\
B&A&-4B\\
0&B&A+4B
\end{pmatrix},
\]

because

\[
\alpha^3=4\alpha^2-4\alpha+2.
\]

Hence

\[
N(A+B\alpha)
 =\det M(A,B)
 =A^3+4A^2B+4AB^2+2B^3.
\]

Taking

\[
A=x-24z^2,\qquad B=18z^2
\]

gives the useful ring certificate

\[
\boxed{
N(\delta)=x^3-432xz^4+8208z^6.
}
\]

The proof below only uses the assumed equality \(N(\delta)=y^2\), not this expanded formula.

### 1.2 The primes \(2,3,11\)

Modulo \(2\),

\[
h(T)\equiv T^3.
\]

Thus

\[
(2)=\mathfrak p_2^3,
\qquad
\mathfrak p_2=(2,\alpha),
\qquad
f(\mathfrak p_2/2)=1,
\qquad
N\mathfrak p_2=2.
\]

Modulo \(3\),

\[
h(T)\equiv T^3+2T^2+T+1.
\]

Its values at \(0,1,2\) are respectively \(1,2,1\), so it has no root in
\(\mathbf F_3\). Being cubic, it is irreducible. Therefore

\[
(3)=\mathfrak p_3
\]

is inert, with

\[
f(\mathfrak p_3/3)=3,
\qquad
N\mathfrak p_3=3^3=27.
\]

Modulo \(11\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
h(T)
&\equiv (T-8)^2(T-10)\\
&\equiv (T+3)^2(T+1).
\end{aligned}
\]

Consequently

\[
\boxed{
(11)=\mathfrak p_8^2\mathfrak p_{10},
}
\]

where

\[
\mathfrak p_8=(11,\alpha-8),
\qquad
\mathfrak p_{10}=(11,\alpha-10),
\]

and

\[
f(\mathfrak p_8/11)=f(\mathfrak p_{10}/11)=1.
\]

This is the ramified split case that must not be glossed over.

# 2. Let a prime ideal divide \((\delta)\)

Let \(\mathfrak P\) be a nonzero prime ideal with

\[
\mathfrak P\mid(\delta).
\]

Write

\[
\mathfrak P\cap\mathbf Z=(p)
\]

for the rational prime below \(\mathfrak P\). Since ideal divisibility gives
\((\delta)\subseteq\mathfrak P\), we have

\[
\delta\in\mathfrak P.
\]

Because \(y\ne0\) and \(N(\delta)=y^2\), the element \(\delta\) is nonzero, so all ideal valuations below are finite natural numbers.

## 2.1 First lemma: \(p\nmid z\)

Suppose \(p\mid z\). Then \(z\in\mathfrak P\), hence

\[
z^2(-24+18\alpha)\in\mathfrak P.
\]

Since

\[
\delta=x+z^2(-24+18\alpha)\in\mathfrak P,
\]

we obtain \(x\in\mathfrak P\). As \(x\in\mathbf Z\), this means

\[
x\in\mathfrak P\cap\mathbf Z=(p),
\]

so \(p\mid x\). This contradicts \(\gcd(x,z)=1\). Therefore

\[
\boxed{p\nmid z.}
\]

This argument works for every rational prime \(p\), though it is needed below only when \(p\ne2,3\).

# 3. The cases \(p=2\) and \(p=3\)

## 3.1 The prime \(2\)

Let \(k_{\mathfrak P}=\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak P\). In this field, reduction of
\(h(\alpha)=0\) modulo \(2\) gives

\[
\bar\alpha^3=0.
\]

A field has no nonzero nilpotents, so

\[
\bar\alpha=0.
\]

Thus every prime over \(2\) contains \((2,\alpha)\). But

\[
\mathcal O_K/(2,\alpha)\cong\mathbf F_2,
\]

so \((2,\alpha)\) is maximal. Hence it is the unique prime over \(2\):

\[
\mathfrak P=\mathfrak p_2=(2,\alpha),
\qquad f(\mathfrak P/2)=1.
\]

## 3.2 The prime \(3\)

Because \(h\bmod3\) is irreducible,

\[
\mathcal O_K/(3)
 \cong
\mathbf F_3[T]/(T^3+2T^2+T+1)
\]

is a field. Thus \((3)\) itself is the unique prime over \(3\), and

\[
\mathfrak P=(3),
\qquad f(\mathfrak P/3)=3.
\]

Both residue degrees are odd.

# 4. The case \(p\ne2,3\): fixed residue and uniqueness

Set

\[
c=x-24z^2,
\qquad
d=18z^2,
\qquad
\delta=c+d\alpha.
\]

By the previous lemma, \(p\nmid z\). Since \(p\ne2,3\), we have

\[
p\nmid d.
\]

Choose integers \(m,n\) satisfying

\[
md+np=1.
\]

Define the integer residue

\[
r=-mc.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
r\equiv(24z^2-x)(18z^2)^{-1}\pmod p.
\]

Now the following exact identity is the Lean-friendly heart of the proof:

\[
\boxed{
\alpha-r=m\delta+np\alpha.
}
\]

Indeed,

\[
m\delta+np\alpha
 =mc+(md+np)\alpha
 =mc+\alpha
 =\alpha-r.
\]

Both terms on the right belong to \(\mathfrak P\), so

\[
\alpha-r\in\mathfrak P.
\]

Thus the residue of \(\alpha\) in \(\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak P\) is the fixed rational residue \(r\in\mathbf F_p\).

## 4.1 Residue degree one

Because

\[
\mathcal O_K=\mathbf Z[\alpha],
\]

every element of \(\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak P\) is a polynomial in \(\bar\alpha\) with coefficients in \(\mathbf F_p\). But \(\bar\alpha=r\in\mathbf F_p\). Therefore the canonical map

\[
\mathbf F_p\longrightarrow\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak P
\]

is surjective, hence an isomorphism. Consequently

\[
\boxed{f(\mathfrak P/p)=1.}
\]

This conclusion remains valid at the ramified prime \(p=11\).

## 4.2 Uniqueness among primes dividing \((\delta)\)

Since \(h(\alpha)=0\) and \(\alpha\equiv r\pmod{\mathfrak P}\), we have

\[
h(r)\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

Define

\[
\mathfrak m_r=(p,\alpha-r).
\]

Using \(\mathcal O_K=\mathbf Z[\alpha]\) and \(h(r)\equiv0\pmod p\), evaluation at \(r\) gives

\[
\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak m_r\cong\mathbf F_p.
\]

Hence \(\mathfrak m_r\) is maximal. We have \(\mathfrak m_r\subseteq\mathfrak P\), so maximality gives

\[
\mathfrak P=\mathfrak m_r.
\]

Now let \(\mathfrak Q\) be any other prime over \(p\) with
\(\mathfrak Q\mid(\delta)\). The same integers \(m,n,r\) work for \(\mathfrak Q\), because they depend only on \(p,x,z\). Thus

\[
\alpha-r\in\mathfrak Q,
\]

and the same maximality argument yields

\[
\mathfrak Q=\mathfrak m_r=\mathfrak P.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
\text{for }p\ne2,3,\text{ at most one prime over }p\text{ divides }(\delta).
}
\]

This is the precise uniqueness statement needed by the norm argument.

# 5. Explicit verification of the \(p=11\) case

There are two primes over \(11\):

\[
\mathfrak p_8=(11,\alpha-8),
\qquad
\mathfrak p_{10}=(11,\alpha-10),
\]

with

\[
(11)=\mathfrak p_8^2\mathfrak p_{10}.
\]

For a prime over \(11\) dividing \((\delta)\), the fixed residue \(r\) must satisfy

\[
h(r)=0\quad\text{in }\mathbf F_{11}.
\]

The only roots are \(8\) and \(10\). Hence:

- if \(r=8\), the only dividing prime is \(\mathfrak p_8\);
- if \(r=10\), the only dividing prime is \(\mathfrak p_{10}\);
- if \(r\notin\{8,10\}\), no prime over \(11\) divides \((\delta)\).

In particular, \(\mathfrak p_8\) and \(\mathfrak p_{10}\) cannot both divide \((\delta)\).

The ramification index

\[
e(\mathfrak p_8/11)=2
\]

does not enter the norm exponent of an arbitrary ideal. For a prime ideal,

\[
N(\mathfrak P)=p^{f(\mathfrak P/p)},
\]

so the contribution of \(\mathfrak P^{n}\) to the rational norm is

\[
p^{f(\mathfrak P/p)n}.
\]

Thus the contribution at \(\mathfrak p_8\) is \(11^n\), not \(11^{2n}\).

# 6. Exact norm-factorization parity argument

Factor the nonzero principal ideal in the Dedekind domain \(\mathcal O_K\):

\[
(\delta)=\prod_{\mathfrak Q}\mathfrak Q^{n_{\mathfrak Q}},
\qquad
n_{\mathfrak Q}=v_{\mathfrak Q}(\delta)\in\mathbf Z_{\ge0}.
\]

Only finitely many \(n_{\mathfrak Q}\) are nonzero.

For a rational prime \(p\), taking absolute ideal norms gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
v_p\bigl(N((\delta))\bigr)
 &=v_p\left(
   \prod_{\mathfrak Q}N(\mathfrak Q)^{n_{\mathfrak Q}}
   \right)\\
 &=\sum_{\mathfrak Q\mid p}
   f(\mathfrak Q/p)n_{\mathfrak Q}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Since \((\delta)\) is principal,

\[
N((\delta))=\left|N_{K/\mathbf Q}(\delta)\right|=y^2.
\]

Therefore

\[
\boxed{
2v_p(|y|)
 =\sum_{\mathfrak Q\mid p}
   f(\mathfrak Q/p)n_{\mathfrak Q}.
}
\tag{*}
\]

Now fix \(\mathfrak P\mid(\delta)\), and let \(p\) be its rational prime below.

### If \(p=2\)

There is only one prime above \(2\), and its residue degree is \(1\). Equation \((*)\) becomes

\[
2v_2(|y|)=n_{\mathfrak P}.
\]

Hence \(n_{\mathfrak P}\) is even.

### If \(p=3\)

There is only one prime above \(3\), and its residue degree is \(3\). Equation \((*)\) becomes

\[
2v_3(|y|)=3n_{\mathfrak P}.
\]

The left side is even and \(3\) is odd, so \(n_{\mathfrak P}\) is even.

### If \(p\ne2,3\)

The fixed-residue argument proves that \(\mathfrak P\) is the only prime over \(p\) having positive valuation in \((\delta)\), and

\[
f(\mathfrak P/p)=1.
\]

Equation \((*)\) becomes

\[
2v_p(|y|)=n_{\mathfrak P},
\]

so \(n_{\mathfrak P}\) is even.

Thus, for every prime ideal \(\mathfrak P\),

\[
\boxed{v_{\mathfrak P}(\delta)\equiv0\pmod2.}
\]

---

# 7. Constructing the square root ideal

Write

\[
n_{\mathfrak P}=2m_{\mathfrak P}.
\]

Define

\[
\mathfrak a=\prod_{\mathfrak P}\mathfrak P^{m_{\mathfrak P}}.
\]

The product is finite because only finitely many \(n_{\mathfrak P}\) are nonzero. Then

\[
\mathfrak a^2
 =\prod_{\mathfrak P}\mathfrak P^{2m_{\mathfrak P}}
 =\prod_{\mathfrak P}\mathfrak P^{n_{\mathfrak P}}
 =(\delta).
\]

This proves

\[
\boxed{(\delta)=\mathfrak a^2.}
\]

---

## 8.1 Prime multiplicity

For a height-one prime, use the multiplicity already present in Mathlib's Dedekind factorization API:

```lean
def idealMult (P : IsDedekindDomain.HeightOneSpectrum OK)
    (I : Ideal OK) : ℕ :=
  (Associates.mk P.asIdeal).count (Associates.mk I).factors
```

The relevant existing factorization theorem is

```lean
Ideal.finprod_heightOneSpectrum_factorization
```

and finite support is supplied by

```lean
Ideal.finite_factors
Ideal.hasFiniteMulSupport
```

in `Mathlib.RingTheory.DedekindDomain.Factorization`.

## 8.2 Principal ideal norm

Use

```lean
Ideal.absNorm_span_singleton
```

from `Mathlib.RingTheory.Ideal.Norm.AbsNorm` to obtain

```lean
Ideal.absNorm (Ideal.span ({delta} : Set OK))
  = Int.natAbs (Algebra.norm ℤ delta)
  = Int.natAbs (y ^ 2)
  = (Int.natAbs y) ^ 2
```

The norm of a prime over `p` is given by the inertia-degree theorem

```lean
Ideal.pow_inertiaDeg
```

from `Mathlib.RingTheory.RamificationInertia.Inertia`:

```lean
p ^ P.asIdeal.inertiaDeg ℤ = Ideal.absNorm P.asIdeal
```

for a prime `P` lying over `(p)`.

## 8.4 Lean-friendly fixed-residue lemma

The crucial case can be stated without division in the residue field:

```lean
lemma alpha_sub_fixed_residue_mem
    (P : Ideal OK) [P.IsPrime]
    (hpP : P.comap (algebraMap ℤ OK) =
      Ideal.span ({(p : ℤ)} : Set ℤ))
    (hdelta : delta α x z ∈ P)
    (hp2 : p ≠ 2) (hp3 : p ≠ 3)
    (hpz : ¬ p ∣ z) :
    ∃ m n r : ℤ,
      m * (18 * z ^ 2) + n * p = 1 ∧
      r = -m * (x - 24 * z ^ 2) ∧
      α - algebraMap ℤ OK r ∈ P := by
  obtain ⟨m, n, hmn⟩ :=
    Int.gcd_eq_gcd_ab ... -- Bézout, using `p ∤ 18*z^2`
  refine ⟨m, n, -m * (x - 24 * z ^ 2), hmn, rfl, ?_⟩
  have hmδ : algebraMap ℤ OK m * delta α x z ∈ P :=
    P.mul_mem_left _ hdelta
  -- The target is exactly
  -- `α - r = m*delta + n*p*α`.
  convert P.add_mem hmδ
    (P.mul_mem_left _ (show algebraMap ℤ OK p ∈ P from ...)) using 1 <;>
    ring_nf
```

After this, define

```lean
Jr := Ideal.span ({algebraMap ℤ OK p,
                   α - algebraMap ℤ OK r} : Set OK)
```

and prove

```lean
OK ⧸ Jr ≃+* ZMod p
```

by evaluation at `r`. This gives both:

```lean
P = Jr
P.asIdeal.inertiaDeg ℤ = 1
```

and proves uniqueness of any second prime dividing `(delta)` over the same `p`.

# 9. Audit of the proposed simplification

The exact status of each proposed step is:

- **`p | delta` implies `p ∤ z`: correct.** The contradiction uses the contraction
  \(\mathfrak P\cap\mathbf Z=(p)\).
- **For `p ≠ 2,3`, `18z²` is invertible modulo `p`: correct.**
- **A prime dividing `delta` forces a fixed rational residue for `alpha`: correct.** The Bézout identity above avoids field division.
- **That prime has residue degree one: correct, using `O_K = Z[alpha]`.**
- **There is a unique prime over `p` dividing `delta`: correct.** It means unique among the dividing primes, not necessarily unique in the whole factorization of `(p)`.
- **At `p=2`, `alpha³=0` forces `alpha=0`: correct, because the residue quotient is a field.** This yields the unique prime and residue degree one.
- **At `p=3`, the cubic is irreducible: correct.** The prime is inert and has residue degree three.
- **The `p=11` ramified split case causes no exception: correct.** There are two primes over `11`, but the fixed residue selects exactly one; both have residue degree one.
- **Square norm then forces every prime-ideal valuation even: correct only after uniqueness is included.** Odd residue degrees by themselves would not suffice if two dividing primes lay over the same rational prime.

Thus the proposed proof is valid after making the uniqueness and norm-exponent steps explicit as above.
