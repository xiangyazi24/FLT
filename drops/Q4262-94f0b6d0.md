\[
E(\mathbf Q)\cong \mathbf Z/2\mathbf Z\oplus\mathbf Z/4\mathbf Z,
\]

with exactly the eight points

\[
O,\ (0,0),\ (15,0),\ (16,0),\ (12,\pm12),\ (20,\pm20).

One wording correction: full rational \(2\)-torsion gives **three** nontrivial order-\(2\) kernels and hence three degree-\(2\) quotient isogenies. For the standard \(2\)-isogeny descent one chooses the kernel \(\langle(0,0)\rangle\) and then uses that isogeny together with its dual; these are the two maps relevant below.

# 1. Full rational \(2\)-torsion

The cubic splits completely:

\[
X(X-15)(X-16)=0.
\]

Therefore

\[
E[2](\mathbf Q)=
\{O,T_0,T_{15},T_{16}\},
\quad
T_0=(0,0),\ T_{15}=(15,0),\ T_{16}=(16,0),
\]

and the three nonzero points are distinct, so

\[
E[2](\mathbf Q)\cong(\mathbf Z/2\mathbf Z)^2.
\]

For reference, the three quotient models obtained by translating the chosen kernel point to \(x=0\) are:

- kernel \(T_0\): \(y^2=x^3+62x^2+x\);
- kernel \(T_{15}\): \(y^2=x^3-28x^2+256x\);
- kernel \(T_{16}\): \(y^2=x^3-34x^2+225x\).

Only the first is needed for the proposed descent.

# 2. The \(2\)-isogeny with kernel \((0,0)\)

Write

\[
E:\quad y^2=x^3+a x^2+b x
\]

with

\[
a=-31,\qquad b=240.
\]

For the quotient by \(\langle(0,0)\rangle\), the standard formula is

\[
E':\quad y^2=x^3-2a x^2+(a^2-4b)x.
\]

Here

\[
-2a=62,\qquad a^2-4b=31^2-960=961-960=1.
\]

Thus

\[
oxed{E':y^2=x^3+62x^2+x},\qquad
oxed{a'=62,\ b'=1}.
\]

For \(x\ne0\), the isogeny itself may be written

\[
\phi(x,y)=
\left(\frac{y^2}{x^2},
      \frac{y(b-x^2)}{x^2}\right).
\]

For example,

\[
\phi(12,12)=(1,8),
\]

and \(8^2=1+62+1\).

## 2.1 Kummer quartic

For a squareclass \(d\), choose coprime integers \(M,e\), with \(e>0\), and put

\[
x=d\frac{M^2}{e^2},\qquad
 y=d\frac{MN}{e^3}.
\]

Substitution into \(y^2=x^3+a x^2+b x\) gives exactly

\[
oxed{N^2=dM^4+aM^2e^2+\frac bd e^4}.
\]

For \(E\), this is

\[
N^2=dM^4-31M^2e^2+\frac{240}{d}e^4. \tag{E_d}
\]

Up to squares, a representative may be chosen from

\[
d\in\{\pm1,\pm2,\pm3,\pm5,\pm6,\pm10,\pm15,\pm30\}.
\]

### Negative \(d\) are impossible

For every real point of \(E\), one has \(x\ge0\): for \(x<0\), all three factors
\(x,x-15,x-16\) are negative, so their product is negative.

For a nonzero point represented by the quartic, \(\operatorname{sign}(x)=\operatorname{sign}(d)\). Hence only positive \(d\) occur. The special point \((0,0)\) maps to the class of \(b=240\), namely \(15\), also positive.

Thus only

\[
1,2,3,5,6,10,15,30
\]

remain.

## 2.2 Exclusion of every even \(d\)

Let

\[
d\in\{2,6,10,30\}.
\]

Then \(240/d\equiv8\pmod{16}\), and \(d\equiv2,6,10,14\pmod{16}\).
Because \(\gcd(M,e)=1\), they are not both even.

### Case A: \(M,e\) both odd

Modulo \(8\), odd fourth powers and odd squares are \(1\), while \(240/d\equiv0\pmod8\). Since \(-31\equiv1\pmod8\),

\[
N^2\equiv d+1\pmod8.
\]

For \(d\equiv2\) or \(6\pmod8\), this is \(3\) or \(7\pmod8\), never a square.

### Case B: \(M\) even and \(e\) odd

Modulo \(16\),

\[
dM^4\equiv0,\qquad
\frac{240}{d}e^4\equiv8,\qquad
-31M^2e^2\equiv0\text{ or }4.
\]

Therefore

\[
N^2\equiv8\text{ or }12\pmod{16},
\]

again impossible.

### Case C: \(M\) odd and \(e\) even

Modulo \(16\),

\[
\frac{240}{d}e^4\equiv0,\qquad
-31M^2e^2\equiv0\text{ or }4,\qquad
 dM^4\equiv d.
\]

Hence \(N^2\equiv d\) or \(d+4\pmod{16}\). The four possibilities are

\[
\{2,6\},\quad\{6,10\},\quad\{10,14\},\quad\{14,2\},
\]

none of which is a quadratic residue modulo \(16\).

Thus

\[
oxed{d=2,6,10,30\text{ are all excluded}}.
\]

## 2.3 The four surviving classes and exact witnesses

The remaining classes all have primitive integral witnesses:

| \(d\) | \((M,e,N)\) | Quartic check | corresponding point |
|---:|---:|---|---|
| \(1\) | \((4,1,0)\) | \(0=4^4-31\cdot4^2+240=256-496+240\) | \((16,0)\) |

Changing the sign of \(N\) changes the sign of \(y\), so the witnesses for \(d=3,5\) also produce \((12,-12)\) and \((20,-20)\).

Consequently

\[
oxed{\alpha(E(\mathbf Q))=\{1,3,5,15\}}.
\]

This claimed image is correct.

# 3. The dual Kummer image on \(E'\)

For

\[
E':y^2=x^3+62x^2+x,
\]

we have \(a'=62,\ b'=1\). Therefore the only possible squareclasses are

\[
d=1,-1.
\]

The quartic is

\[
N^2=dM^4+62M^2e^2+\frac1d e^4. \tag{E'_d}
\]

## \(d=1\) survives

Take

\[
(M,e,N)=(1,1,8).
\]

Then

\[
8^2=1+62+1=64,
\]

corresponding to the point

\[
(1,8)\in E'(\mathbf Q).
\]

## \(d=-1\) is impossible

The equation becomes

\[
N^2=-M^4+62M^2e^2-e^4. \tag{*}
\]

Again \(\gcd(M,e)=1\).

- If \(M,e\) have opposite parity, then modulo \(8\) the right side of (*) is \(7\), impossible for a square.
- If \(M,e\) are both odd, then odd fourth powers are \(1\pmod{16}\). Also every product of two odd squares is \(1\) or \(9\pmod{16}\), and
  
  \[
  62\cdot1\equiv62\cdot9\equiv14\pmod{16}.
  \]
  
  Hence
  
  \[
  N^2\equiv-1+14-1\equiv12\pmod{16},
  \]
  
  again impossible.

Therefore

\[
oxed{\alpha'(E'(\mathbf Q))=\{1\}}.
\]

This claimed image is also correct.

# 4. Integral model at \(2\)

Use

\[
X=4x+12,\qquad Y=8y+4x+4.
\]

Since

\[
Y=4(2y+x+1)
\]

and

\[
X(X-15)(X-16)
 =16(x+3)(x-1)(4x-3),
\]

the original equation is equivalent to

\[
(2y+x+1)^2=(x+3)(x-1)(4x-3).
\]

Expanding and dividing by \(4\) gives

\[
oxed{E_0:y^2+xy+y=x^3+x^2-5x+2}.
\]

Thus

\[
a_1=1,\quad a_2=1,\quad a_3=1,\quad a_4=-5,\quad a_6=2.
\]

The standard intermediate invariants are

\[
\begin{aligned}
b_2&=a_1^2+4a_2=5,\\
b_4&=2a_4+a_1a_3=-9,\\
b_6&=a_3^2+4a_6=9,\\
b_8&=a_1^2a_6+4a_2a_6-a_1a_3a_4+a_2a_3^2-a_4^2=-9.
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore

\[
\begin{aligned}
\Delta
&=-b_2^2b_8-8b_4^3-27b_6^2+9b_2b_4b_6\\
&=-25(-9)-8(-9)^3-27(9)^2+9(5)(-9)(9)\\
&=225+5832-2187-3645\\
&=225.
\end{aligned}
\]

Hence

\[
oxed{\Delta(E_0)=225}.
\]

For comparison, the original nonminimal equation has

\[
\Delta=2^{12}\cdot225=921600,
\]

consistent with the change of variables having \(u=2\).

Since \(225\equiv1\pmod2\), \(E_0\) has good reduction at \(2\).

## Exact count over \(\mathbf F_2\)

Modulo \(2\), the equation is

\[
y^2+xy+y=x^3+x^2+x.
\]

- \(x=0\): both \(y=0,1\) work;
- \(x=1\): the equation reduces to \(y=1\), so only \(y=1\) works;
- add the point at infinity.

Thus

\[
E_0(\mathbf F_2)=
\{O,(0,0),(0,1),(1,1)\},
\qquad
oxed{\#E_0(\mathbf F_2)=4}.
\]

Moreover the negation law is

\[
-(x,y)=(x,-y-a_1x-a_3)=(x,y+x+1)
\]

in characteristic \(2\). It swaps \((0,0)\) and \((0,1)\), while \((1,1)\) is self-inverse. Hence

\[
oxed{E_0(\mathbf F_2)\cong\mathbf Z/4\mathbf Z},
\]

so its exponent is exactly \(4\). Therefore \(4P\) enters the \(2\)-adic formal kernel for every \(P\in E(\mathbf Q_2)\), exactly as required by the separatedness proof.

# 5. What happens at \(p=5\)

This is the incorrect part of the proposed proof.

Because

\[
5\mid\Delta(E_0)=225,
\]

reduction at \(5\) is **bad**, not good. Thus one cannot use the number of points on the reduced cubic as a good-reduction injective bound on all rational torsion.

## Literal point count on the singular cubic

Modulo \(5\),

\[
y^2+xy+y=x^3+x^2+2.
\]

The affine solutions are

\[
(0,1),\ (0,3),\ (1,4),\ (2,1),
\]

plus \(O\). Therefore the projective cubic has

\[
oxed{5\text{ points over }\mathbf F_5}.
\]

The point

\[
(2,1)
\]

is singular: both partial derivatives vanish there. Under the original coordinates it is the node \((X,Y)=(0,0)\) of

\[
Y^2=X^2(X-1)\pmod5.
\]

So the statement “\(\#E_0(\mathbf F_5)=5\)” is a correct count of the singular projective cubic, but it is **not** the cardinality of an elliptic-curve group and is unusable as the proposed good-reduction torsion bound.

More precisely, \(c_4=241\not\equiv0\pmod5\) and \(v_5(\Delta)=2\), so the reduction is multiplicative of type \(I_2\). The tangent cone at the node is

\[
y^2+x^2=(y-2x)(y+2x),
\]

so it is split multiplicative. The smooth identity component has

\[
\#\mathbf G_m(\mathbf F_5)=4,
\]

and the component group has order \(2\); the corresponding Néron finite quotient has order \(8\), not \(5\).

# 6. Correct good-prime torsion bound

Use \(p=7\) and \(p=11\), both primes of good reduction.

For the original split form

\[
f(x)=x(x-15)(x-16),
\]

one has

\[
\#E(\mathbf F_p)=p+1+\sum_{x\in\mathbf F_p}\chi(f(x)).
\]

## At \(p=7\)

Here \(15\equiv1\) and \(16\equiv2\), so

\[
f(x)=x(x-1)(x-2).
\]

For \(x=0,1,2,3,4,5,6\), the values are

\[
0,0,0,6,3,4,1.
\]

The Legendre-symbol contributions are

\[
0,0,0,-1,-1,1,1,
\]

whose sum is \(0\). Hence

\[
oxed{\#E(\mathbf F_7)=7+1=8}.
\]

## At \(p=11\)

Here \(15\equiv4\) and \(16\equiv5\). The values of \(x(x-4)(x-5)\) for \(x=0,\dots,10\) are

\[
0,1,1,6,0,0,1,9,8,4,3.
\]

The Legendre-symbol contributions are

\[
0,1,1,-1,0,0,1,1,-1,1,1,
\]

whose sum is \(4\). Therefore

\[
oxed{\#E(\mathbf F_{11})=11+1+4=16}.
\]

The usual two-good-prime argument now yields

\[
|E(\mathbf Q)_{\rm tors}|\mid\gcd(8,16)=8.
\]

There are already eight explicit rational points. Also

\[
2(12,12)=(16,0),\qquad
2(20,20)=(16,0),
\]

so \((12,12)\) and \((20,20)\) have order \(4\). Together with the full rational \(2\)-torsion, these points form a subgroup of order \(8\), isomorphic to

\[
\mathbf Z/2\mathbf Z\oplus\mathbf Z/4\mathbf Z.
\]

Thus

\[
E(\mathbf Q)_{\rm tors}
=
\{O,(0,0),(15,0),(16,0),(12,\pm12),(20,\pm20)\}.
\]

Combining this with the \(2\)-isogeny descent rank computation gives the exact rational point set.
